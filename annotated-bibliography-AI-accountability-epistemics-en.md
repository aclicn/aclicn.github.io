# Annotated bibliography: AI accountability and epistemic risks in academic writing and publishing

Compiled: 2026-09-10

The starting papers are van Zoonen, Morgan-Thomas and Tursunbayeva (2026) and Tang (2025). Their shared questions concern who can answer for scholarly claims and whether researchers retain their own judgment and understanding when AI enters knowledge production. The papers are grouped into four themes and ranked within each theme; priority papers are marked **[Essential reading]**.

The original bibliography uses APA 7 and reports that author and publication metadata came from Crossref and arXiv, and that DOI and retraction checks were conducted through Scite. Those checks were not repeated for this language split. The correction to Gerlich (2025) and the erratum to Kaebnick et al. (2023) remain noted. Summaries follow official abstracts where available. For five papers (Lin 2024; Messeri and Crockett 2024; Lee et al. 2025; Brown and Marsden 2026; Colangelo and Galli 2026), the original source notes incomplete abstract access and reliance on prior understanding or brief abstracts. Entry-level caveats are retained; check the full text before citing.

The ranking reflects the compiler's judgment about relevance to the starting papers, foundational importance within a theme, and breadth of citation. It is not an objective measure.

These symbols describe the available files and existing acquisition records, not present-day publisher access. Included files may be preprints or author manuscripts. Every paper has a DOI link.

## Theme A: Accountability, authorship, and journal governance

Related to van Zoonen et al.'s argument for claim accountability.

### A0. Starting paper

✅ van Zoonen, W., Morgan-Thomas, A., & Tursunbayeva, A. (2026). Beyond AI disclosure: Claim accountability and responsible research in scholarly publishing. *European Management Journal*, *44*(4), 550–556. [DOI: 10.1016/j.emj.2026.06.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.emj.2026.06.001)

- Argues that prohibition, mandatory disclosure and validity-only permissiveness all regulate tool use rather than the defensibility of claims; the unit of governance should be the claim, with a named human able to reconstruct and defend it. Proposes a two-threshold framework (confidentiality; judgment) and role-based self-assessments for authors, reviewers, editors and publishers.

### A1. [Essential reading]

❌ Lindebaum, D., & Fleming, P. (2024). ChatGPT undermines human reflexivity, scientific responsibility and responsible management research. *British Journal of Management*, *35*(2), 566–575. [DOI: 10.1111/1467-8551.12781](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8551.12781)

- The core interlocutor for van Zoonen et al. Unpacks ChatGPT's intrinsic epistemological limits: it generates text through high-probability, derivative choices and has no stake in the knowledge it produces, making it prone to irresponsible output, whereas human thinking is embodied, contextual and committed. Concludes that ChatGPT use is incompatible with scientific responsibility and responsible management research.

### A2. [Essential reading]

❌ Hosseini, M., Resnik, D. B., & Holmes, K. (2023). The ethics of disclosing the use of artificial intelligence tools in writing scholarly manuscripts. *Research Ethics*, *19*(4), 449–465. [DOI: 10.1177/17470161231180449](https://doi.org/10.1177/17470161231180449)

- The foundational paper on disclosure ethics. Argues bans are unenforceable and encourage hidden use; because LLMs lack free will they cannot bear moral or legal responsibility, so neither authorship nor acknowledgement is appropriate — they should be cited as tools. Recommends disclosing prompts and affected text in the introduction or methods, recording user, model version and time, and submitting interaction logs as supplementary material.

### A3. [Essential reading]

✅ Lin, Z. (2024). Towards an AI policy framework in scholarly publishing. *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, *28*(2), 85–88. [DOI: 10.1016/j.tics.2023.12.002](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2023.12.002)

- (not based on the official abstract) A policy commentary in the cognitive-science community. Reviews publisher and journal AI policies, identifies inconsistencies and limitations, and calls for a coherent, pragmatic, enabling framework that differentiates disclosure requirements by type and degree of AI use while anchoring accountability firmly with human authors. The most directly relevant piece for cognitive-science researchers.

### A4.

✅ Crawford, J., Purvis, A., Grieve, A., & Taylor, L. (2026). Authorship statement for generative artificial intelligence: Assuring trust and accountability. *Journal of University Teaching and Learning Practice*, *23*(1). [DOI: 10.53761/v16abt43](https://doi.org/10.53761/v16abt43)

- A journal-level enactment of claim accountability. Notes that GenAI blurs the boundaries between human contribution, tool-assisted editing and tool-generated content, creating a veracity problem. Sets out six commitments, including specific disclosure, prohibition of GenAI producing the substantive scholarly contribution, human centrality and confidentiality in review, mandatory reflexivity when GenAI enters methods or analysis, and non-transferability of accountability away from named authors.

### A5.

✅ Binz, M., Alaniz, S., Roskies, A., Aczel, B., Bergstrom, C. T., Allen, C., Schad, D., Wulff, D., West, J. D., Zhang, Q., Shiffrin, R. M., Gershman, S. J., Popov, V., Bender, E. M., Marelli, M., Botvinick, M. M., Akata, Z., & Schulz, E. (2025). How should the advancement of large language models affect the practice of science? *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *122*(5), e2401227121. [DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2401227121](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2401227121)

- A structured debate among four groups. Schulz et al. see working with LLMs as no different from human collaboration; Bender et al. argue LLMs are overhyped and misused and favour specialised, interpretable tools; Marelli et al. stress transparent attribution and responsible use; Botvinick and Gershman insist humans retain responsibility for the scientific roadmap. Each group replies to the others. Well suited to graduate seminar discussion.

### A6.

✅ Johnson, R. F. (2026). Provenance, not prohibition: A framework for AI in scholarly publishing. *Laryngoscope Investigative Otolaryngology*, *11*(4), e70487. [DOI: 10.1002/lio2.70487](https://doi.org/10.1002/lio2.70487)

- An independent proposal closest to van Zoonen's position. The central risk is loss of provenance rather than tool use: readers, reviewers and editors must be able to trace how claims, citations, analyses and interpretations were produced and verified. Four principles: separate capability from responsibility, prioritise provenance over polish, avoid unverifiable bans, and pair disclosure with verification. Governance should target the feedback loop around the manuscript and bind all roles equally.

### A7.

✅ Kaebnick, G. E., Magnus, D. C., Kao, A., Hosseini, M., Resnik, D., Dubljević, V., Rentmeester, C., Gordijn, B., & Cherry, M. J. (2023). Editors' statement on the responsible use of generative AI technologies in scholarly journal publishing. *Hastings Center Report*, *53*(5), 3–6. [DOI: 10.1002/hast.1507](https://doi.org/10.1002/hast.1507) (erratum: [DOI: 10.1002/hast.1534](https://doi.org/10.1002/hast.1534); also published in *American Journal of Bioethics*, [DOI: 10.1080/15265161.2023.2292437](https://doi.org/10.1080/15265161.2023.2292437))

- A joint statement by bioethics and humanities journal editors. Acknowledges that GenAI may both threaten and serve the goals of scholarly work, and offers a preliminary, reasoned set of recommendations for authors, reviewers and editors to foster a wider conversation.

### A8.

❌ Flanagin, A., Bibbins-Domingo, K., Berkwits, M., & Christiansen, S. L. (2023). Nonhuman "authors" and implications for the integrity of scientific publication and medical knowledge. *JAMA*, *329*(8), 637–639. [DOI: 10.1001/jama.2023.1344](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2023.1344)

- The JAMA editorial issued after ChatGPT appeared as a bylined author, establishing the early baseline that nonhuman entities cannot be authors and that AI use must be disclosed. One of the most cited policy documents; useful as historical context.

### A9.

❌ LaFlamme, M., & Meyers, N. (2026). Enacting AI disclosure in scholarly publishing. *American Ethnologist*, *53*(2), 132–135. [DOI: 10.1111/amet.70084](https://doi.org/10.1111/amet.70084)

- A commentary from anthropology sketching emerging AI disclosure and attribution practices, adapting them to the discipline's epistemic and ethical commitments, and situating disclosure expectations within broader regulatory and professional trends. Useful for thinking about how disclosure is enacted across disciplines.

### A10.

✅ Colangelo, M. T., & Galli, C. (2026). The sophist in the server. *EMBO Reports*, *27*(5), 1098–1102. [DOI: 10.1038/s44319-026-00711-w](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44319-026-00711-w)

- (based on a brief abstract) LLMs can defend a hypothesis and its opposite with equal eloquence, creating a new risk of confusing rhetorical plausibility with evidential support. Analyses how LLMs reshape creativity, judgment and responsibility in research and how scientists can use their fluency without surrendering epistemic discipline.

### A supplement: Empirical surveys of journal policies (ranked by relevance)

❌ Ganjavi, C., Eppler, M. B., Pekcan, A., Biedermann, B., Abreu, A., Collins, G. S., Gill, I. S., & Cacciamani, G. E. (2024). Publishers' and journals' instructions to authors on use of generative artificial intelligence in academic and scientific publishing: Bibliometric analysis. *BMJ*, *384*, e077192. [DOI: 10.1136/bmj-2023-077192](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-077192)

- A cross-sectional bibliometric study of GenAI guidance from the top 100 publishers and top 100 journals. Only 24% of publishers but 87% of journals provided guidance; among those, 96–98% prohibited AI authorship, yet only one journal explicitly banned GenAI in manuscript generation. The standard reference for what journals actually require.

✅ Perkins, M., & Roe, J. (2024). Academic publisher guidelines on AI usage: A ChatGPT supported thematic analysis. *F1000Research*, *12*, 1398. [DOI: 10.12688/f1000research.142411.2](https://doi.org/10.12688/f1000research.142411.2)

- An inductive thematic analysis, run both with and without AI assistance, of leading publishers' policies. Consensus: human authorship remains paramount and GenAI use is permissible but must be disclosed; also reflects on the limits and biases of AI-assisted analysis itself.

✅ Raitskaya, L., & Tikhonova, E. (2025). The 2025 landscape of generative AI in scholarly writing and publishing: A scoping review of uses and ethical approaches. *Journal of Language and Education*, *11*(4), 5–51. [DOI: 10.17323/jle.2025.29876](https://doi.org/10.17323/jle.2025.29876)

- A PRISMA-ScR scoping review of 56 publications from 2025. Discourse clusters around governance, technological impact and risk mitigation; identifies emerging normative frameworks such as tiered disclosure models and task-based AI taxonomies (e.g., GAIDeT).

✅ Yoo, J. H. (2025). Defining the boundaries of AI use in scientific writing: A comparative review of editorial policies. *Journal of Korean Medical Science*, *40*(23), e187. [DOI: 10.3346/jkms.2025.40.e187](https://doi.org/10.3346/jkms.2025.40.e187)

- Compares statements from international editors' organisations and major journals' submission policies, and evaluates JKMS's own policy (tool name, prompt, purpose, scope). Discusses the unreliability of AI-detection tools and argues for a culture of responsible disclosure over detection.

❌ Nabavi, A., Safari, F., Shmoury, A. H., Tabet, S., Perdomo-Luna, C., & Celi, L. A. (2026). Artificial intelligence in scholarly peer review: A scoping review of applications, risks, and governance challenges. *International Journal of Medical Informatics*, *214*, 106418. [DOI: 10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2026.106418](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijmedinf.2026.106418)

- A scoping review of 189 records distinguishing assistive (triage, assistance) from autonomous (review generation, prediction) AI in peer review. Key risks: confidentiality breaches when manuscripts go to third-party tools, algorithmic bias favouring elite institutions or male authors, and homogenisation of scholarly voice. Maps directly onto van Zoonen's confidentiality threshold.

## Theme B: Epistemic risks — illusions of understanding and trust calibration

Related to Tang's first concern: epistemic detriment and illusions of understanding.

### B0. Starting paper

✅ Tang, B. L. (2025). The epistemic downside of using LLM-based generative AI in academic writing. *Publications*, *13*(4), 63. [DOI: 10.3390/publications13040063](https://doi.org/10.3390/publications13040063)

- Setting ethics violations aside, argues that LLM use in academic writing carries three intrinsic epistemic pitfalls: epistemic detriment via illusions of understanding, potential cognitive dulling, and habitual or emotional AI dependency — which call for restraint beyond regulation.

### B1. [Essential reading]

❌ Messeri, L., & Crockett, M. J. (2024). Artificial intelligence and illusions of understanding in scientific research. *Nature*, *627*(8002), 49–58. [DOI: 10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07146-0)

- (not based on the official abstract) The direct source of Tang's "illusion of understanding". Surveys scientists' visions of AI (as oracle, surrogate, quant, arbiter) and argues that their appeal — greater productivity and objectivity — comes with illusions of explanatory depth, exploratory breadth and objectivity, risking scientific monocultures. The most cited paper on the epistemic risks of AI in science.

### B2. [Essential reading]

✅ Hila, A. (2025). The epistemological consequences of large language models: Rethinking collective intelligence and institutional knowledge. *AI & Society*, *41*(1), 79–97. [DOI: 10.1007/s00146-025-02426-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-025-02426-3)

- Develops a theory of "collective epistemology" distinguishing internalist (reflective) from externalist (reliabilist) justification. Argues LLMs approximate reliabilism but lack reflective justification, so outsourcing reflective knowledge to them at scale erodes comprehension and justificatory standards collectively; proposes epistemic norms at individual, institutional and legislative tiers. The philosophical paper closest to Tang's argument.

### B3.

✅ Loru, E., Nudo, J., Di Marco, N., Santirocchi, A., Atzeni, R., Cinelli, M., Cestari, V., Rossi-Arnaud, C., & Quattrociocchi, W. (2025). The simulation of judgment in LLMs. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, *122*(42), e2518443122. [DOI: 10.1073/pnas.2518443122](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518443122)

- Benchmarks six LLMs against expert ratings and human judgments under an identical evaluation procedure. Despite aligned outputs, models rely on different observable criteria — lexical associations and statistical priors rather than contextual reasoning — showing political asymmetries and a tendency to confuse linguistic form with epistemic reliability, termed *epistemia*: the illusion of knowledge when surface plausibility replaces verification.

### B4.

❌ Heersmink, R., de Rooij, B., Clavel Vázquez, M. J., & Colombo, M. (2024). A phenomenology and epistemology of large language models: Transparency, trust, and trustworthiness. *Ethics and Information Technology*, *26*(3), 41. [DOI: 10.1007/s10676-024-09777-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10676-024-09777-3)

- Conceptualises LLMs as multifunctional computational cognitive artifacts. Phenomenologically they can be experienced as a "quasi-other" and anthropomorphised; they are opaque in data and algorithm yet phenomenologically and informationally transparent. This combination makes trust calibration difficult, producing unwarranted trust (especially when models hallucinate) and undermining trustworthiness.

### B5.

✅ Kim, S. S. Y., Vaughan, J. W., Liao, Q. V., Lombrozo, T., & Russakovsky, O. (2025). Fostering appropriate reliance on large language models: The role of explanations, sources, and inconsistencies. *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, 1–19. [DOI: 10.1145/3706598.3714020](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3714020)

- A think-aloud study followed by a pre-registered controlled experiment (N = 308). Explanations increase reliance on both correct and incorrect responses; reliance on incorrect responses drops only when sources are provided or explanations are inconsistent. An important empirical caution against the intuition that AI reasoning makes outputs more trustworthy.

### B6.

❌ Bottazzi Grifoni, E., & Ferrario, R. (2025). The bewitching AI: The illusion of communication with large language models. *Philosophy & Technology*, *38*(2), 61. [DOI: 10.1007/s13347-025-00893-6](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-025-00893-6)

- Analyses human–LLM interaction through late Wittgenstein. Communication requires constancy of reference points in definitions and judgments, which LLMs cannot maintain across negations and contradictions. Apparent understanding is a "bewitchment" produced by LLMs' statistical adherence to linguistic patterns and humans' tendency to follow familiar language games; better mimicry only deepens the illusion.

### B7.

✅ Abdurahman, S., Atari, M., Karimi-Malekabadi, F., Xue, M. J., Trager, J., Park, P. S., Golazizian, P., Omrani, A., & Dehghani, M. (2024). Perils and opportunities in using large language models in psychological research. *PNAS Nexus*, *3*(7), pgae245. [DOI: 10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae245](https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae245)

- A caution against "GPTology" in psychological research. Demonstrates empirically the risks of treating LLMs (especially zero-shot) as universal text-analysis tools or models of the psyche, stressing global psychological diversity, transparent open methods, and resistance to homogenisation and over-reliance. Most practical for researchers using LLMs for annotation or simulated participants.

### B8.

✅ Maynard, A. D. (2026). *The AI cognitive Trojan horse: How large language models may bypass human epistemic vigilance* [Preprint]. arXiv. [DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2601.07085](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2601.07085)

- Preprint. Drawing on Sperber et al.'s epistemic vigilance, argues the main risk of conversational AI is not error or deception but "honest non-signals": fluency, helpfulness and apparent disinterest that carry information in humans (where they are costly) but are computationally trivial for LLMs. Identifies four bypass mechanisms and speculates that cognitively sophisticated users may be more vulnerable.

### B9.

✅ Ibrahim, L., Collins, K. M., Kim, S. S. Y., Reuel, A., Lamparth, M., Feng, K., Ahmad, L., Soni, P., El Kattan, A., Stein, M., Swaroop, S., Padmakumar, V., Sucholutsky, I., Strait, A., Yang, D., Liao, Q. V., & Bhatt, U. (2025). *Measuring and mitigating overreliance to build human-compatible AI* [Preprint]. arXiv. [DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2509.08010](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2509.08010)

- Preprint. Argues that measuring and mitigating overreliance must be central to LLM research and deployment. Consolidates individual and societal risks (high-stakes errors, governance challenges, cognitive deskilling), examines model characteristics, design features and user biases that drive overreliance, identifies three measurement gaps, and proposes mitigation strategies.

## Theme C: Cognitive dulling, cognitive offloading, and dependency

Related to Tang's second and third concerns: cognitive dulling and AI dependency.

### C1. [Essential reading]

✅ Lee, H.-P., Sarkar, A., Tankelevitch, L., Drosos, I., Rintel, S., Banks, R., & Wilson, N. (2025). The impact of generative AI on critical thinking: Self-reported reductions in cognitive effort and confidence effects from a survey of knowledge workers. *Proceedings of the 2025 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, 1–22. [DOI: 10.1145/3706598.3713778](https://doi.org/10.1145/3706598.3713778)

- (not based on the official abstract) A survey of knowledge workers reporting real GenAI-assisted tasks. Higher confidence in GenAI was associated with less self-reported critical thinking, higher self-confidence with more. GenAI shifts critical thinking from information gathering to verification, from problem-solving to integrating AI responses, and from task execution to task stewardship. Among the most cited empirical studies on AI and critical thinking.

### C2. [Essential reading]

✅ Fan, Y., Tang, L., Le, H., Shen, K., Tan, S., Zhao, Y., Shen, Y., Li, X., & Gašević, D. (2025). Beware of metacognitive laziness: Effects of generative artificial intelligence on learning motivation, processes, and performance. *British Journal of Educational Technology*, *56*(2), 489–530. [DOI: 10.1111/bjet.13544](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13544)

- A randomised experiment with 117 university students supported by ChatGPT, a human expert, writing-analytics tools or nothing. No differences in motivation; significant differences in self-regulated learning processes; the ChatGPT group improved essay scores most but showed no advantage in knowledge gain or transfer. The authors call this decoupling of performance from learning "metacognitive laziness".

### C3.

❌ Gerlich, M. (2025). AI tools in society: Impacts on cognitive offloading and the future of critical thinking. *Societies*, *15*(1), 6. [DOI: 10.3390/soc15010006](https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15010006) (correction: [DOI: 10.3390/soc15090252](https://doi.org/10.3390/soc15090252))

- A mixed-methods study (surveys and interviews, N = 666). Frequent AI-tool use correlated negatively with critical-thinking ability, mediated by cognitive offloading; younger participants showed higher dependence and lower scores; higher education predicted better critical thinking. Very widely cited, but correlational — causal claims should be cautious — and carries a table correction.

### C4.

⚠️ Kosmyna, N., Hauptmann, E., Yuan, Y. T., Situ, J., Liao, X.-H., Beresnitzky, A. V., Braunstein, I., & Maes, P. (2025). *Your brain on ChatGPT: Accumulation of cognitive debt when using an AI assistant for essay writing task* [Preprint]. arXiv. [DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2506.08872](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2506.08872)

- Preprint. Fifty-four participants wrote essays in LLM, search-engine or brain-only conditions over three sessions, with a fourth crossover session (n = 18). EEG showed the strongest, most distributed connectivity in the brain-only group and the weakest in the LLM group; LLM-to-brain participants showed reduced alpha and beta connectivity; LLM users reported the lowest essay ownership and struggled to quote their own work. Framed as "cognitive debt". A methodological commentary exists (Stanković et al., 2026) and should be read alongside.

### C5.

✅ Yan, L., Greiff, S., Lodge, J. M., & Gašević, D. (2025). Distinguishing performance gains from learning when using generative AI. *Nature Reviews Psychology*, *4*(7), 435–436. [DOI: 10.1038/s44159-025-00467-5](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-025-00467-5)

- A short comment arguing that although GenAI boosts learners' performance, such uses do not promote the deep cognitive and metacognitive processing that high-quality learning requires; research and practice must distinguish performance gains from learning. A conceptual distillation of Fan et al.

### C6.

✅ Clark, A. (2025). Extending minds with generative AI. *Nature Communications*, *16*(1), 4627. [DOI: 10.1038/s41467-025-59906-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-59906-9)

- The counter-voice from the extended-mind tradition. Argues it is basic human nature to build hybrid thinking systems that incorporate non-biological resources, and that recognising this reframes both the threats and promises of AI. Included as a balance to the assumption that offloading is inherently harmful.

### C7.

✅ Delikoura, I., Fung, Y. R., & Hui, P. (2025). *From superficial outputs to superficial learning: Risks of large language models in education* [Preprint]. arXiv. [DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2509.21972](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2509.21972)

- Preprint. A systematic review of 70 empirical studies across computer science, education and psychology. Model-level risks (superficial understanding, bias, hallucination, anthropomorphism) cascade through learner interaction into cognitive and behavioural outcomes: reduced neural activity, over-reliance, diminished independent learning and loss of agency. Proposes an LLM-Risk Adapted Learning Model.

### C8.

✅ Choudhury, A., & Chaudhry, Z. (2024). Large language models and user trust: Consequence of self-referential learning loop and the deskilling of health care professionals. *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, *26*, e56764. [DOI: 10.2196/56764](https://doi.org/10.2196/56764)

- Examines clinician trust in LLMs, the self-referential loop in which AI-generated content feeds back into training data, and consequences for competence. Stresses that expert users can offload routine tasks while maintaining critical oversight, and warns that frequent reliance risks deskilling diagnostic and reasoning abilities, particularly for trainees.

### C9.

✅ Izquierdo-Condoy, J. S., Arias-Intriago, M., Tello-De-la-Torre, A., Busch, F., & Ortiz-Prado, E. (2025). Generative artificial intelligence in medical education: Enhancing critical thinking or undermining cognitive autonomy? *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, *27*, e76340. [DOI: 10.2196/76340](https://doi.org/10.2196/76340)

- A viewpoint assessing GenAI's effect on critical thinking in medical education. Evidence is mixed: greater engagement alongside passivity and susceptibility to misinformation. Argues that without curricular integration emphasising ethical use, prompt engineering and critical evaluation, GenAI may compromise students' cognitive autonomy.

## Theme D: Academic writing practices

### D1. [Essential reading]

✅ Bekker, M. (2024). Large language models and academic writing: Five tiers of engagement. *South African Journal of Science*, *120*(1/2). [DOI: 10.17159/sajs.2024/17147](https://doi.org/10.17159/sajs.2024/17147)

- Breaks LLM use in academic writing into five tiers of engagement, offering practical guidance for cautious adoption. Its tiered logic maps directly onto van Zoonen's graded judgment threshold, making it the easiest piece to translate into lab or course norms.

### D2.

✅ Xu, Z., Qiu, Y., Sun, L., Miao, F., Wu, F., Li, X., Wang, X., Lu, H., Zhang, Z., Hu, Y., Li, J., Jin, L., Zhang, F., Luo, R., Liu, X., Li, Y., & Liu, J. (2026). *GhostCite: A large-scale analysis of citation validity in the age of large language models* [Preprint]. arXiv. [DOI: 10.48550/arxiv.2602.06718](https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.2602.06718)

- Preprint. Builds an open-source citation-verification framework and reports three experiments: 13 LLMs hallucinate citations at 14–95% across 40 domains; among 2.2 million citations from top AI/ML and security venues (2020–2025), 1.07% of papers contain invalid or fabricated references, up 80.9% in 2025 alone; a researcher survey finds 41.5% copy BibTeX unchecked and 76.7% of reviewers do not thoroughly check references.

### D3.

❌ Fecher, B., Hebing, M., Laufer, M., Pohle, J., & Sofsky, F. (2023). Friend or foe? Exploring the implications of large language models on the science system. *AI & Society*, *40*(2), 447–459. [DOI: 10.1007/s00146-023-01791-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00146-023-01791-1)

- A Delphi study with 72 researchers in AI and digitisation. LLMs show transformative potential in administrative, creative and analytical tasks, but risks of bias, misinformation and quality assurance require proactive regulation and science education. An early, still frequently cited system-level analysis.

### D4.

✅ Bozkurt, A. (2024). GenAI et al.: Cocreation, authorship, ownership, academic ethics and integrity in a time of generative AI. *Open Praxis*, *16*(1), 1–10. [DOI: 10.55982/openpraxis.16.1.654](https://doi.org/10.55982/openpraxis.16.1.654)
